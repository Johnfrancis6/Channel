# Script — 01_uber "Uber Was Cheap. Who Paid?" (S04 eval run)

## Cold open

"In 2008, on a snowy night in Paris, Garrett Camp and I had trouble getting a cab."[^F01] That's how Travis Kalanick tells it. Two friends, one cold street, no taxi. And one question from Camp, as Kalanick remembers it: "Why can't we just tap a button and get a ride?"[^F02]

You know the rest. You tap, a car shows up, and the ride costs less than you expected. But it was cheap for a reason. A transport analyst named Hubert Horan went through the figures Uber gave its own investors, and he came back with an estimate that sounds like a typo. Passengers were paying only 41 percent of what their trips actually cost.[^F05]

So who paid the rest? Not you. Somebody else covered the gap, ride after ride. This is the story of who paid for your cheap ride.

## Setup

Uber launched in San Francisco on May 31, 2010.[^F04] And here's the funny part: it did not start as a cheap ride at all. Back in Paris, the two friends had spent a week at a tech conference called LeWeb, and Camp's first idea was a limo timeshare service.[^F03] A shared driver. A shared luxury car. A treat, not a bargain.

The bargain came later. And it came with a bill that nobody showed you at the time.

Horan's numbers cover the year ending September 2015. He wrote them up on a finance blog called Naked Capitalism. In that year, by his count, Uber lost $2 billion on revenue of $1.4 billion.[^F06] Read that again, slowly. For every dollar that came in from riders, more than two dollars went out.

Now, young companies lose money all the time. That part is normal. What made Uber different, in Horan's reading, was that the low fare was the strategy. He calls it a subsidy, which means money spent so that you pay less than the ride really costs. You got the ride. Someone else got the bill.

Think about what that does to a city. If a ride costs you less than it costs to make, you take more rides. Your friends do too. The taxi on the corner can't match that price, because the taxi has to pay for itself. Uber didn't, at least not yet.

So this video follows the dollar you did not pay. Three stops: the investors who sent it, the drivers who earned it, and the taxi owners who lost it.

Who pays? Let's find out.

## The decision

Every subsidy needs a sponsor. Somebody has to decide, again and again, to send the money that keeps your fare low. For Uber, one of those decisions was made on June 1, 2016.[^F07]

That day, Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund put $3.5 billion into Uber.[^F07] Not a loan: an investment, for a piece of the company. It was the kind of number that ends arguments in a boardroom.

Picture the trade from both sides. On one side, a startup that loses money on rides and needs more of it to keep going. On the other, a state fund with money to place, looking for the next big thing. The fund got a slice of Uber. Uber got time. And the fund's managing director, Yasir Al Rumayyan, got a seat on Uber's board.[^F08]

There's a detail in that deal worth slowing down for. The investment kept Uber's valuation at $62.5 billion.[^F07] A valuation is what investors agree the whole company is worth, which means it's a bet on the future, not a count of today's profits. Uber had no profits to count. The price said: we believe the losses will stop one day, and the company will own the road when they do.

That belief is the real decision in this story. As long as people kept sending money to cover the gap, Kalanick did not have to charge you more. Every cheap ride you took in those years was partly paid for by someone far away who had never sat in your car. It's worth pausing on how strange that is. A ride across your own town, paid for in part by a fund in a country you may never visit. You never saw that money. You only felt it, every time the fare came in low.

Was that a bad deal for you? Honestly, no. You got a ride for less than it cost. Was it a gift? Not quite either. Money like that comes with a plan, and the plan was to grow first and charge later.

But a plan like that has a weak spot. It only works as long as somebody keeps paying. So let's follow that money, and see who ended up holding it.

## Follow the dollar

### Step 1: the investors

Start with the people who sent the money. For years, they mostly watched it go out.

By the end of 2018, Uber's accumulated deficit was about $7.9 billion.[^F13] That's a dry phrase for a simple idea: since it began, Uber had lost $7.9 billion more than it had earned. When the company prepared to sell its shares to the public, it had to say so in writing, in plain sight, near the top of its risks: "We have incurred significant losses since inception, including in the United States and other major markets."[^F13]

By then the man from that snowy night in Paris was gone. Travis Kalanick resigned as chief executive in June 2017.[^F11] In August 2017, Uber named Dara Khosrowshahi, the head of Expedia, as its new boss.[^F12] His job was to turn a money-losing machine into a business.

In May 2019, Uber held its IPO, which is when a company first sells its shares to anyone who wants them. The price was $45 a share, and the sale raised $8.1 billion.[^F14] On the first day of trading, the shares closed below that price. Not a crash. Just a shrug. Khosrowshahi put a brave face on it: "It was a tough week to go public, but we got it done."[^F14]

One analyst, Matt Kennedy, said the quiet part out loud: "Unlike Facebook, however, Uber does not make money."[^F14]

So the first payer is clear. Investors paid billions to keep your fare low. But they paid with their eyes open, hoping for a big return. The next group did not get a choice.

### Step 2: the drivers

Every cheap ride has a driver in the front seat. And the driver needed to believe the job was worth it.

Uber told people who were thinking of driving that uberX drivers in New York made more than $90,000 a year.[^F10] That's a number that gets you to quit a job. The Federal Trade Commission looked at the same drivers and found something smaller. The median was more like $61,000, and only 10 percent of drivers earned the $90,000 Uber had advertised.[^F10]

In January 2017, Uber agreed to pay $20 million to settle the charges.[^F09] It did not admit the allegations.[^F09] Jessica Rich, who ran consumer protection at the commission, put it in one line: "Many consumers sign up to drive for Uber, but they shouldn't be taken for a ride about their earnings potential."[^F09]

Yes, she really said "taken for a ride."

So here is the second payer: the driver, sold a number most drivers never reached. But there was a third group, and they paid the most.

### Step 3: the taxi owners

In New York, a yellow cab can't just drive. It needs a medallion, a permit the city limits, and owners bought them like you'd buy a house.

Before Uber and Lyft took over the streets, a medallion was worth about $1 million.[^F15] People borrowed to buy one. They planned to pay it off fare by fare, and retire on what it was worth.

Then the price rose to more than $1 million and crashed in late 2014.[^F16] Imagine paying off a house that loses most of its value while you still owe the bank for it. That's what happened to a lot of drivers who owned their medallion. By 2019, more than 950 medallion owners had filed for bankruptcy.[^F16]

The fight lasted years. In 2021, New York taxi drivers held a 15-day hunger strike.[^F17] Fifteen days without food. Afterward, the city announced a program to lower their debt payments.[^F17]

Was Uber the only cause? No. The medallion loans have their own story, and it's not a simple one. But the cheap ride and the crashing medallion happened on the same streets, at the same time. Somebody's cheap fare was somebody else's lost retirement.

That's the third payer, and the hardest one to see from the back seat.

## Consequences

So who won, who lost, and what changed?

Start in London. Two drivers, Mr Aslam and Mr Farrar, went to court with a simple question: are we working for Uber, or just using its app?[^F18] Uber said they were independent. The details of their work told a different story.

In the London cases, Uber set the fare, took 20 percent of it, and paid the rest to the driver.[^F19] The driver brought the car, the fuel and the hours. Uber brought the app and kept a fifth. And the app kept score. Passengers rated the driver from 1 to 5 after each trip, and a driver whose average fell too low got warnings, then could be cut off.[^F21] A driver who turned down or cancelled too many trip requests was logged off for ten minutes.[^F20] Ten minutes of no work, as a penalty, for someone Uber called independent. In 2016, around 40,000 Uber drivers were working in the UK, around 30,000 of them in London,[^F22] so the answer mattered to a lot of people.

On February 19, 2021, the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom answered. Unanimously. The drivers are workers, with the right to the national minimum wage and to paid annual leave.[^F18] The court looked at who set the fares, who wrote the terms and who handed out penalties, and it came to a blunt conclusion about the drivers: "the only way in which they can increase their earnings is by working longer hours."[^F18]

That ruling changed the deal for drivers in that country. It didn't erase the years before it, and it didn't reach drivers anywhere else. Each country, and often each city, has had to fight the same question on its own.

And the investors? They waited a long time. In 2022, Uber still lost $9.1 billion, and $1.8 billion on its operations alone.[^F23] Then the turn came. In 2023, Uber made $1.1 billion from its operations,[^F24] its first full year of operating profit.[^F24] Operating profit means the money the business itself makes before things like taxes and investment gains, so it's the cleanest sign the rides finally paid for themselves.

Khosrowshahi called it a milestone: "2023 was an inflection point for Uber."[^F23] By then, the app was powering nearly 26 million trips a day on average.[^F25]

But keep the nuance. The 41 percent was one analyst's estimate, made from numbers Uber gave investors, not from audited accounts. Uber never had to agree with it. And the company that lost billions is also the one you still open on a rainy night. Both things are true.

So who won? Riders, for years. Investors who held on, eventually. Who lost? Drivers who earned less than they were told, and taxi owners who were left holding a medallion and a loan.

## Lesson and next

Here's the pattern, and it goes far beyond Uber. When something costs you less than it costs to make, the difference doesn't vanish. Someone covers it. Sometimes it's an investor betting on later. Sometimes it's a worker who has less room to say no. Sometimes it's a business that was there before, and can't match a price that was never meant to last.

Remember that snowy night in Paris. Two friends wanted a ride at the tap of a button. They got it, and so did you. What nobody said, that night or for years after, was that the button had a price, and that the price would be split among people you would never meet: a fund on the other side of the world, a driver in the front seat, a cab owner with a loan.

So next time something feels too cheap, ask the question this channel is named after. Whose dime is it?
